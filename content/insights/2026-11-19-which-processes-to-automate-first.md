The best first automation is rarely the most impressive one. It's a process that happens often, wastes real time, follows clear rules and won't cause harm if it occasionally needs a human to step in. This article gives you a simple way to find it.

## Why does the choice of first process matter so much?

Because the first automation sets the team's opinion of every one after it. A quick, reliable win builds trust and frees time. A complex, fragile first project that breaks every few weeks makes people wary of the next idea, however good.

There is a second reason. Automation makes a process run faster, including its flaws. If a process is unclear or full of workarounds, automating it first just produces mistakes more quickly. That's why the method below asks you to fix the process before you automate it.

## What are the five scoring factors?

Score each candidate process from 1 (low) to 5 (high) on these five factors. They are our working method, not an industry standard, but they cover the questions that most often decide whether an automation pays off.

| Factor | Score 5 when… | Score 1 when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Volume** | It runs many times a day or week | It runs a few times a year |
| **Time** | Each run takes real effort | Each run takes a minute |
| **Rules** | The steps are the same every time and written down | Every case needs judgement |
| **Digital inputs** | Inputs arrive as emails, forms, files or system records | Inputs are paper, phone calls or memory |
| **Low risk** | Mistakes are easy to spot and cheap to fix | Mistakes hit customers, money or compliance |

Add the five scores for a total out of 25. Anything above 18 is usually a strong first candidate. Between 12 and 18, it's worth a closer look. Below 12, park it for now.

## What does a worked example look like?

Here are three processes a typical services business might score:

| Process | Volume | Time | Rules | Digital | Low risk | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Logging supplier invoices into accounts | 5 | 4 | 4 | 5 | 3 | **21** |
| Routing website enquiries to the right person | 4 | 2 | 5 | 5 | 5 | **21** |
| Writing tailored proposals for new clients | 2 | 5 | 1 | 4 | 2 | **14** |

Invoices and enquiry routing are good first candidates. Proposals take a lot of time, but each one needs judgement, so AI is better used there as a drafting assistant with a person in charge than as a fully automated step.

## How do you break a tie?

When two processes score the same, pick the one with:

1. **A willing owner** who wants it fixed and will test it.
2. **A clear measure**, such as hours per week or time to respond, that you can record before and after.
3. **Fewer systems involved.** A process that lives in one or two tools is faster to automate and easier to maintain.

## Should you fix the process before automating it?

Yes, almost always. Before building anything:

- **Write the steps down** as they happen today, including the workarounds.
- **Remove steps that add nothing**, such as copying the same data twice or approvals nobody reads.
- **Agree the exceptions**: what happens when an input is missing or unusual, and who handles it.
- **Decide where a person stays involved.** For anything touching customers, money or legal commitments, keep a human review step, at least at first.

Often this clean-up saves time on its own, before any automation is built.

## Which tools should you use?

Start with what you already have. Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace both include workflow tools, and platforms such as Make, Zapier and n8n connect common business apps without custom code. AI models can now handle steps that used to need a person, such as reading an invoice, sorting an email or drafting a reply, but they still make mistakes, so design for review and easy correction.

Custom AI agents that act across several systems are worth considering once simpler automations are working well and you understand where judgement is needed.

## What should you measure after go-live?

Track four things for the first month:

| Measure | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Hours saved per week | The main benefit, measured against your baseline |
| Exceptions handled by people | Shows where the rules or inputs need work |
| Errors caught after the fact | Tells you whether the review step is in the right place |
| Team satisfaction | An automation people work around isn't saving time |

## FAQ

### What is the easiest business process to automate first?

Usually one that runs often, follows the same steps each time and starts from a digital input, such as routing website enquiries, logging invoices or sending standard follow-up emails. Score your own candidates on volume, time, rules, digital inputs and risk to find yours.

### Should we automate a process that isn't working well today?

Not yet. Automation makes a process run faster, flaws included. Write the steps down, remove what adds nothing and agree how exceptions are handled first, then automate the cleaned-up version.

### Do we need AI to automate business processes?

Not always. Many first automations are simple rules that connect two apps. AI helps when a step involves reading or writing text, such as extracting data from documents or drafting replies, and it works best with a person reviewing the output.

### How long does a first automation take to build?

Simple workflows between common apps can often be built and tested in days. Processes that span several systems or need careful exception handling take longer, which is one more reason to start with a simple, high-scoring process.
