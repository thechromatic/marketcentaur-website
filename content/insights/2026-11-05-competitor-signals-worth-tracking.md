Once you start watching competitors, the problem flips. You stop wondering what they're doing and start drowning in alerts. The fix is to know in advance which signals tend to mean something and which ones rarely do.

Here's the list we use as a starting point. Adjust it for your market.

## Signals worth tracking

### Pricing and packaging

**1. Pricing page changes.** A new tier, a removed plan, a feature moved between tiers, or a switch from "contact us" to published prices. Pricing pages change less often than blog pages, so a change there is usually a deliberate decision.

**2. A new free plan or extended trial.** This often signals a push into smaller customers, or pressure to show user growth.

**3. Discounting in public.** Promotions on a product that used to be sold at list price can point to a slow quarter or an attempt to win share quickly.

### Go-to-market

**4. Job postings, especially by location.** A first sales hire in a new country, a cluster of partner-manager roles, or a shift from engineering to sales hiring all hint at the next 12 months.

**5. New partners, resellers or marketplace listings.** These reveal which channels a competitor is betting on and which regions they're trying to reach.

**6. Local-language pages or local currency.** Translating a website is effort. It rarely happens without a plan to sell in that market.

**7. Event sponsorships.** Where a competitor spends event budget shows which industries and regions they're prioritising.

### Product and messaging

**8. Homepage headline changes.** When the main promise changes, the positioning has changed. Compare the new headline with the old one and ask who it's now written for.

**9. New case studies.** The industries and company sizes featured in fresh case studies show who they're winning and who they want to attract next.

**10. Integrations announced.** New integrations show which ecosystems they want to be part of, and sometimes which customers they're chasing.

### Money and leadership

**11. Funding rounds and acquisitions.** New money usually means faster hiring, more marketing spend and sometimes lower prices. An acquisition often signals a new product line or market.

**12. Senior hires.** A new head of a region, a new chief marketing officer or a new partnerships lead often comes before a visible change in strategy.

## Signals that are mostly noise

**1. Social media follower counts.** They move slowly, can be bought, and rarely connect to revenue.

**2. Routine blog posts.** Content marketing schedules produce posts every week. Read the topics in aggregate, not one by one.

**3. Generic award announcements.** Many awards are pay-to-enter. Treat them as marketing, not market signals.

**4. Small website design tweaks.** New colours or images rarely mean a change in direction. Wording changes matter far more than design changes.

**5. Individual reviews.** One angry or glowing review tells you little. Patterns across many reviews over months are worth watching; single reviews are not.

## How to tell signal from noise

Ask three questions about anything that comes in:

- **Did it cost them real effort or money?** Hiring, translating, pricing changes and partnerships do. Tweets don't.
- **Is it a change, or more of the same?** Track differences from last month, not activity.
- **Does it affect a decision we're making?** If it doesn't touch your pricing, positioning, markets or pipeline, log it and move on.

Signals that pass all three deserve a line in your weekly brief, with a short note on what it might mean for you and what you could do about it.
