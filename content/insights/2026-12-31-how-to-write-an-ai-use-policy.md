Most teams already use AI at work, whether or not there is a policy. In the Microsoft and LinkedIn 2024 Work Trend Index, 75% of knowledge workers said they use generative AI at work, and 78% of those users bring their own tools rather than ones their employer provides ([Microsoft](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2024/05/08/microsoft-and-linkedin-release-the-2024-work-trend-index-on-the-state-of-ai-at-work/)). The same survey found that only 39% of AI users had received AI training from their company.

So the question is not whether to allow AI. It is how to make the use you already have safe, consistent and worth the time. A short, plain policy does most of that work. This guide sets out what to put in it, how to write it in a week and how to make it stick.

## What is an AI use policy?

An AI use policy is a short internal document that tells staff which AI tools they may use, for what, with which data, and who checks the output. It is not a strategy paper or a legal contract. Think of it as the AI equivalent of your expenses policy: clear rules for everyday decisions, plus a route to ask when something is unclear.

It also gives you something to point to. ISO/IEC 42001:2023, the international standard for AI management systems, expects leadership to set an AI policy and define roles, and its controls include a policy for developing and using AI systems ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/iso-standard-offers-ai-management-system-template)). In the EU, the AI Act asks organisations that use AI to take measures to support AI literacy among their staff; our guide to [the EU AI Act dates](/insights/eu-ai-act-what-applies-2-december-2026) covers what that means in practice. A written policy plus short training is a proportionate way to show you have done so.

## What should the policy cover?

Keep it to seven sections. Each answers one question a member of staff will actually have.

| Section | The question it answers | Example rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Scope | Who and what does this cover? | All staff, contractors and agencies working for us, on any device used for work |
| 2. Approved tools | Which tools can I use? | Use the tools on the approved list with your work account; ask before adding a new one |
| 3. Data rules | What can I put in? | Never enter customer personal data, passwords, unreleased financials or client confidential material into a tool that is not approved for it |
| 4. Human review | When must a person check the output? | Anything sent to a customer, published, or used in a decision about a person is reviewed by a named person first |
| 5. Disclosure | When do we tell people AI was used? | Chat and voice assistants say they are automated; realistic images of people are labelled |
| 6. Prohibited uses | What must I never do? | No AI-only decisions on hiring, pay or credit; no impersonating real people |
| 7. Ownership and review | Who owns this, and how do I ask? | Named owner, a contact for questions, quarterly review |

The data rules matter most, because that is where the real risk sits. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology's Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024) lists twelve risks that generative AI creates or makes worse, including confabulation (confident but false output), data privacy, information security and intellectual property ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence)). Your data and review rules are the practical answer to those four.

## How do you write it in a week?

Five steps, each small enough for a busy team.

1. **List what people already use.** Send a two-question survey: which AI tools do you use for work, and for what? Add what IT can see from sign-ins and expense claims. This becomes your first AI-use register.
2. **Sort tools into three groups.** Approved (work accounts, settings checked, data terms reviewed), allowed for public information only, and not allowed. Most teams end up with a short approved list.
3. **Write the data rules as examples.** "Do not enter confidential data" is easy to agree with and hard to apply. Give five or six real examples from your own work: a customer email thread, a draft contract, a payroll export, a public blog post. Mark each yes or no.
4. **Name the reviewers.** For each type of output that leaves the building (customer replies, proposals, marketing copy, code), say whose job it is to check it. Review means reading for accuracy and tone, not skimming.
5. **Test the draft with three people.** Ask someone in sales, operations and finance to apply it to a real task this week. Fix every point where they hesitated.

Write in plain sentences. If a rule needs a paragraph of explanation, it is probably two rules.

## What makes a policy actually work?

A policy that nobody reads changes nothing. In our experience, three things decide whether it is followed (this is our view, drawn from the teams we work with):

- **It makes the safe route the easy route.** If the approved tool is slower or harder to reach than a personal account, people will use their personal account. Provide work accounts for the tools people need.
- **It comes with training on real tasks.** An hour of practice on your own documents beats a slide deck on AI risk. Show the data rules in action and let people try the approved tools.
- **It has a named owner and a quick way to ask.** People should know who to message when a new tool appears or a case is unclear, and get an answer within days, not weeks.

Then keep the register current. Each time a tool is added or a new use starts, add a line: tool, purpose, data used, reviewer. That register is also what you will need if a client, auditor or regulator asks how you use AI.

## How does this connect to privacy law?

An AI use policy sits next to your privacy obligations rather than replacing them. If you handle personal data of people in India, the DPDP Rules bring most duties into force on 13 May 2027; our [DPDP readiness guide](/insights/dpdp-what-to-finish-before-may-2027) sets out what to finish first. In the EU and UK, data protection law already applies to personal data entered into AI tools. Your data rules should match what your privacy notices say you do with personal data. This is a practical summary, not legal advice; your own counsel decides what applies.

## FAQ

### How long should an AI use policy be?

One to two pages for most teams. Put the rules people need every day on the first page, and move background, definitions and the tool list to an appendix or the register.

### Should we ban personal AI accounts for work?

A blanket ban tends to push use out of sight. A better rule is to provide work accounts for the tools people need and limit personal accounts to public information only.

### Who should own the AI use policy?

One named person, usually in operations, IT or legal, with input from the teams that use AI most. The owner keeps the register, answers questions and runs the quarterly review.

### How often should we review it?

Every quarter, and whenever a major tool, law or use changes. A short review that updates the approved list and the examples is enough.

### Does a small business need an AI use policy?

Yes, if staff use AI tools with customer or company data. A small business can start with a one-page version and a simple register, then add detail as use grows.
