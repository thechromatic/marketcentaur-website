Large companies pay analysts to watch competitors. Most growing B2B companies don't have that luxury, so competitor tracking happens in bursts: before a board meeting, after a lost deal, or when someone happens to spot a competitor's ad. By then the move has already landed.

The good news is that almost everything worth knowing about a competitor is public. The hard part isn't access. It's attention and interpretation. Here's a lightweight system a one-person marketing team can run.

## 1. Decide what you're watching for

Not every competitor move matters. Start with the decisions your business will make in the next six months, such as pricing, a new market, a launch or a hiring plan, and work backwards to the signals that would affect them. The useful categories are:

- **Pricing and packaging:** new tiers, free plans, discounts, changes to what's included
- **Product:** launches, retired features, new integrations
- **Go-to-market:** new regions, partners, resellers, event sponsorships
- **Hiring:** the roles and locations in their job postings
- **Messaging:** changes to homepage headlines, target industries, case studies
- **Money and leadership:** funding, acquisitions, executive hires

## 2. Keep the list short

Track three to five direct competitors closely and a wider ring of about ten loosely. Trying to watch twenty companies equally means watching none of them well.

## 3. Set up collection in one afternoon

- Google Alerts for each competitor's name, plus their name with words like "launches", "partners" and "pricing"
- Subscriptions to their blogs, newsrooms and newsletters
- A page-change monitor on their pricing, product and careers pages (several tools have free tiers)
- A simple spreadsheet with four columns: date, competitor, what changed, source link

## 4. Review weekly, not constantly

Book a fixed 30-minute slot each week. Scan what came in, delete the noise, and log what's left. Checking constantly leads to reacting to everything. A weekly rhythm lets patterns show.

## 5. Always answer "so what?"

This is the step that turns information into intelligence. For each logged item, write one line on why it matters to you and one possible response. For example, if a competitor adds a free tier, your entry plan may come under pressure, so it may be time to review your trial or starter offer.

If you can't write a "so what", the item probably doesn't belong in your log.

## 6. Share it in a format people read

A short weekly note covering the top three moves, why they matter and suggested responses gets read. A 40-link digest doesn't. Send it to sales, product and leadership, and ask for one reply: "What are customers saying about these?" Front-line teams often know things no public source shows.

## 7. Use AI for the reading, not the judging

AI tools are now good at the tedious parts: summarising articles, spotting duplicates, sorting signals into categories and drafting the first version of your note. They're less reliable at judging what matters to your business, and they can confidently state things that aren't in the source. Keep a person on the "so what", and check every claim against its link.

## How do you start this week?

Pick three competitors and set up alerts and page monitors for them today. Book 30 minutes next Friday to review. After a month, you'll have a record of their moves and a much clearer picture of where they're heading.

## FAQ

### How many competitors should a small team track?

Three to five direct competitors is enough for most small teams. Add others to a lighter watchlist that you check monthly.

### How much time does lightweight competitive intelligence take?

About an afternoon to set up alerts and page monitors, then roughly 30 minutes a week to review changes and write the summary.

### Can AI run competitive intelligence for you?

AI is good at reading and summarising pages, news and job ads quickly. A person should still decide what a change means and how to respond.
