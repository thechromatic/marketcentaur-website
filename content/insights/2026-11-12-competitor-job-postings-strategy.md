Press releases are written to impress. Job postings are written to hire. That difference makes them one of the most honest public signals a company sends. To attract the right candidate, a company has to describe what it is building, where, and why, often months before any of it is announced.

Here's how to read them.

## Why are job postings worth watching?

Hiring is expensive and slow, so companies only open roles they've already decided to fund. A cluster of new postings usually reflects a decision that has already been made at budget level. That makes job ads an early signal, not a reaction.

They're also specific. A posting has to say which team, which location, which tools and which customers the person will work with. That detail is exactly what's missing from most marketing copy.

## Five things to look for

### 1. Where they're hiring

Location is the clearest signal. A first sales or customer-success hire in a new country usually means a market entry is planned. A "founding" or "first" role in a region is an even stronger hint. Watch for:

- New countries or cities appearing in their careers page
- Language requirements ("fluent Japanese", "Bahasa Indonesia a plus")
- Remote roles restricted to a specific time zone

### 2. Which functions are growing

The mix of roles shows priorities. A company hiring mostly engineers is building product. One hiring mostly sellers and partner managers is pushing distribution. A wave of customer-success roles can mean they're focused on retention, or that churn has become a problem.

### 3. The tools and skills they ask for

Required skills reveal the technology stack and the direction of the product. A sudden appearance of machine-learning, payments or compliance skills in engineering roles often points to a new capability. Requests for experience with a specific CRM or industry platform hint at which ecosystems they want to sell into.

### 4. The customers they mention

Many postings describe the customers the role will serve: "enterprise accounts", "mid-market manufacturers", "public sector". If that language shifts over a few months, their target market is shifting too.

### 5. Seniority

Senior hires come before strategy changes. A new head of a region, a vice president of partnerships or a first product marketing lead for a new segment tends to arrive a few months before the visible move.

## How to collect them without much effort

- **Monitor their careers page** with a page-change tool, so you're alerted when roles are added or removed.
- **Set alerts on job boards** for the competitor's name. Most large boards support saved searches with email alerts.
- **Keep a simple log:** date, role, location, function, and one line on what it might mean.

Monthly is usually enough. Hiring patterns develop over weeks, not days.

## How to interpret what you find

A single posting can mean almost anything: a replacement hire, an experiment, or a role that never gets filled. Look for patterns instead:

- **Several roles in the same new location** within a quarter
- **A function that keeps growing** while others stay flat
- **New skills that repeat** across multiple postings

Then ask the same question you'd ask of any signal: does this affect a decision we're making? If a competitor is building a sales team in a market you're about to enter, that changes your timing and your messaging. If they're hiring heavily in an area you don't compete in, note it and move on.

## A few cautions

- **Postings stay up after roles are filled.** Check how long a role has been listed.
- **Some companies post roles to build a candidate pipeline,** not because they're hiring now.
- **Job titles vary.** "Growth", "demand" and "acquisition" can describe the same job at different companies.
- **Treat it as evidence, not proof.** Combine hiring signals with others, such as new partners, local-language pages or pricing changes, before drawing firm conclusions.

## Start this month

Pick your three closest competitors and read through every open role on their careers pages. Note the locations, functions and customers mentioned. Repeat next month and compare. The differences will tell you more about their plans than their last three press releases.

## FAQ

### Where can you find a competitor's job postings?

Check their careers page first, then LinkedIn Jobs and the job boards common in their industry. Saving each posting helps, since ads are often taken down.

### How far ahead do job postings signal strategy?

Hiring often comes months before a launch or market entry, so postings are one of the earliest public signals available.

### Can you read too much into a single job ad?

Yes. One posting may be a replacement hire. Look for clusters of similar roles or a change from previous months before drawing conclusions.
