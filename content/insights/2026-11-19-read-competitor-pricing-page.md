A pricing page is one of the few places where a company has to make clear choices in public. It has to decide who each plan is for, what's included, what's held back and how much to charge. Read carefully, it tells you a lot about a competitor's strategy.

Here's a step-by-step way to read one.

## 1. Start with who it's written for

Look at plan names and the short description under each one. Names like "Starter", "Team" and "Enterprise" tell you which customer sizes they're targeting. Industry-specific plans tell you which verticals matter enough to package for.

Then look at the plan they highlight, often marked "Most popular" or "Recommended". That's the plan they most want you to buy, and usually the one their business depends on.

## 2. Find the value metric

Every pricing model charges for something: users, usage, projects, locations, contacts or features. This is the value metric, and it reveals what the company believes customers value most.

- **Per-user pricing** suggests a tool used by many people across a team.
- **Usage-based pricing** suggests value grows with volume, and lowers the barrier to starting.
- **Flat pricing by tier** suggests they want simplicity and predictable revenue.

If a competitor changes their value metric, treat it as a major signal. It usually means a change in who they're selling to.

## 3. Look at what's held back

The features reserved for higher tiers are the ones a company thinks people will pay more for. Common examples are integrations, security features, reporting, support levels and admin controls. The line between tiers shows where they think the upgrade happens.

Compare this with your own packaging. If they include something in their entry plan that you charge extra for, your prospects will notice.

## 4. Check "contact us" versus published prices

Hiding prices behind "contact sales" usually means larger deals, longer sales cycles and room to negotiate. Publishing prices usually means a push toward self-serve buying and smaller customers.

A move from hidden to published prices, or the other way, is one of the clearest pricing signals you'll see.

## 5. Read the small print

The details beneath the main table are often the most revealing:

- **Annual versus monthly discounts** show how much they value upfront cash and commitment.
- **Minimum seats or contract lengths** show which customers they're trying to filter out.
- **Overage charges** show where they expect heavy users to pay more.
- **Free plan or trial limits** show how they want new users to experience the product.

## 6. Read the FAQ and comparison table

Pricing FAQs answer the objections their sales team hears most often. If several questions are about switching from another provider, that's who they're competing with. Comparison tables show which features they consider their strengths.

## 7. Track changes over time

One snapshot tells you their current strategy. Changes tell you where it's going. Set up a page-change monitor on their pricing page and keep a dated copy whenever it changes. Over a year, you'll see:

- New plans added for segments they're moving into
- Plans retired from segments they're leaving
- Features moving down tiers as competition increases
- Prices rising where they feel confident, or falling where they're under pressure

## How do you turn it into a decision?

For each change you notice, write one line on what it might mean for you and one possible response. For example: "Competitor moved reporting into their entry plan. Our reporting add-on may face objections. Consider including a basic version in our starter tier."

Pricing pages are public, change rarely and reflect real decisions. That makes them one of the best-value pages to watch.

## FAQ

### What is a value metric in pricing?

The value metric is the unit a company charges on, such as per user, per location or per transaction. It shows what the company believes customers value most.

### Why do some competitors hide their prices?

"Contact us" pricing usually means deals vary widely, the company sells to larger buyers, or it wants a sales conversation before quoting.

### How often do pricing pages change?

Rarely, which is what makes each change meaningful. Monitoring the page monthly, or with an automatic change alert, is usually enough.
