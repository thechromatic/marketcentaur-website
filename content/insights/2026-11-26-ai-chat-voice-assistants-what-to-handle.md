AI chat and voice assistants can answer customers at any hour, in several languages, without making them wait. They can also give a confident wrong answer that your business has to stand behind. The difference comes down to which conversations you give them, and the guardrails around those conversations.

## What should an AI assistant handle?

Conversations that are frequent, factual and low-risk. Good examples:

- **Information questions** answered from your own content: opening hours, locations, services, delivery areas, how a process works.
- **Bookings and appointments**, where the assistant checks real availability in your calendar or booking system.
- **Status updates** such as "where is my order?", using live data from your systems.
- **Routing**: understanding what the customer needs and passing them to the right person or team, with a summary.
- **Collecting details** before a person takes over, such as name, account number, the problem and photos.
- **After-hours cover**, so out-of-hours enquiries are captured and followed up the next morning instead of lost.

## What should stay with people?

Anything where a wrong answer is costly, where judgement or empathy matters, or where the answer creates a commitment:

- Refunds, credits, discounts and exceptions to policy
- Complaints and unhappy customers
- Sensitive situations: bereavement, health, financial difficulty, safety
- Legal, medical or financial advice
- Contract terms, quotes and anything legally binding
- Cases the assistant doesn't recognise or can't resolve after one or two tries

| Conversation | AI assistant | Person |
|---|---|---|
| "Are you open on Sunday?" | Answers from approved content | — |
| "Can I move my appointment to Friday?" | Checks the calendar and rebooks | Steps in if no slot fits |
| "Where is my order?" | Gives live status | Takes over if it's late or lost |
| "I want a refund." | Collects details, explains next steps | Decides and replies |
| "Your product caused a problem." | Acknowledges, collects details, hands over | Handles the complaint |

## Why does this matter? A real case

In February 2024 a Canadian tribunal ruled against Air Canada after its website chatbot told a customer he could apply for a bereavement fare after travelling. The airline's own policy said otherwise. According to the [American Bar Association's summary of Moffatt v. Air Canada](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2024-february/bc-tribunal-confirms-companies-remain-liable-information-provided-ai-chatbot/), the airline argued the chatbot was responsible for its own actions. The tribunal rejected that: the chatbot was part of the airline's website, and the airline was responsible for what it said.

The lesson for any business is simple. Customers treat what your assistant says as what your business says. Plan on that basis.

## What rules apply to AI assistants?

Rules vary by country, so check what applies to you. One clear example: under [Article 50 of the EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/), AI systems that interact directly with people must be designed so that people know they are dealing with AI, unless that's obvious from the context. That obligation applies from 2 August 2026, and it may also matter to businesses outside the EU whose assistants talk to people in the EU.

Even where no law requires it, telling customers they're talking to an AI assistant, and offering a person, is good practice. Our [Governance, Risk & Compliance practice](/practices/ai-governance) can help you work out what applies.

## What guardrails make an AI assistant safe to use?

1. **Ground every answer in approved content.** Connect the assistant to your website, policies and FAQs, and instruct it to say "I don't know" rather than guess.
2. **Disclose that it's AI**, at the start of the conversation.
3. **Make handover easy.** A customer should be able to reach a person at any point, and the person should receive a summary so the customer doesn't repeat themselves.
4. **Limit what it can do.** Give it read access by default, and only the actions it needs, such as booking, never issuing refunds.
5. **Protect personal data.** Collect only what you need, and check where conversations are stored and who can see them.
6. **Review conversations every week.** Read a sample, fix wrong answers at the source and add missing content.
7. **Test before launch** with real questions, including awkward ones, in every language you plan to support.

## How do you measure whether it's working?

| Measure | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Share of conversations resolved without a person | Whether the assistant handles the right conversations |
| Handover rate and reasons | Where content or rules are missing |
| Wrong or unsupported answers found in reviews | Whether grounding and instructions are working |
| Customer satisfaction after the conversation | Whether customers find it helpful, not just fast |
| Enquiries captured out of hours | The value of round-the-clock cover |

## Where should you start?

Start with one channel (usually website chat or your main phone line) and the ten questions customers ask most often. Get those right, review weekly for a month, then widen the scope.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between an AI chatbot and an AI voice assistant?

A chatbot works in text, on a website or in messaging apps such as WhatsApp. A voice assistant answers phone calls and speaks with callers. Both should follow the same rules on what they handle, disclosure and handover.

### Can an AI assistant handle customer complaints?

It can acknowledge the complaint, collect the details and pass them to a person with a summary. The complaint itself should be handled by a person, who can use judgement and make decisions.

### Is a business responsible for what its AI chatbot says?

In the Moffatt v. Air Canada case, a Canadian tribunal held the airline responsible for its chatbot's wrong answer. Plan on the basis that customers will treat your assistant's answers as your business's answers.

### Do we have to tell customers they are talking to AI?

In the EU, Article 50 of the AI Act requires it for systems that interact with people, unless it's obvious from the context, from 2 August 2026. Elsewhere, check local rules; disclosure is good practice either way.
