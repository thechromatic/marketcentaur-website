Question: Which mid-size freight forwarders should we target this quarter, who decides, and what should we say?
Sample report. Tallis Freight Systems is a fictional company, and every account, figure and finding below is illustrative. It shows the structure, depth and labelling of a real deliverable, not results for a real client. In client work, every observation links to its public source.

1. Bottom line

25accounts researched and ranked against the agreed ideal-customer profile
7accounts with a buying signal in the last 90 days
3segments, each with its own outreach angle
  • Mid-size forwarders expanding into new lanes are the best fit. They outgrow spreadsheets and email first, and the people who feel it are easy to identify.
  • Timing signals matter more than size. Seven accounts posted roles or announcements that point to new systems work this quarter.
  • Outreach should lead with customs-paperwork time, not features. It is the pain named most often in the accounts' own job ads.

So what: Start with the seven "now" accounts in tier A, and lead with the customs-paperwork angle for segment 1.

Observed Agreed in the kickoff call, from Tallis's eight best current customers.

5. Competitor snapshot

Observed Three competitors named most often in target accounts' job ads and case studies (anonymized).

CompetitorPositioningRecent move
Competitor XAll-in-one platform for large forwardersRaised its minimum contract size
Competitor YLow-cost customs moduleLimited Dutch-language support

Interpretation Competitor X's larger contracts leave mid-size forwarders under-served. That gap is Tallis's opening.

6. Outreach angles by segment

  • Recommendation Segment 1, UK forwarders adding lanes: "New lanes usually mean more customs paperwork per shipment. Here's how two forwarders your size cut entry time without a big-bang migration."
  • Recommendation Segment 2, Dutch 3PLs expanding warehouses: lead with Dutch-language support and a short implementation, where Competitor Y is weak.
  • Recommendation Segment 3, new operations leaders: offer a 30-minute process review in their first 90 days, when change is expected.

7. Confidence and caveats

  • Fit scores apply the agreed criteria to public information. They rank accounts; they don't predict a purchase.
  • Signals are dated. Anything older than 90 days moves an account to tier B until refreshed.
  • Role maps describe likely structures from public sources. Your team should confirm them in early conversations.
