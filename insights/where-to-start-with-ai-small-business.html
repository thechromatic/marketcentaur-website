Where Should a Small Business Start with AI? A 30-Day Plan

A practical 30-day plan for starting with AI in a small or mid-sized business: pick one problem, set ground rules, run a small pilot and measure it.

By The MarketCentaur Team12 November 20265 min read
Short answer

Start with one costly, repetitive problem rather than a tool. In 30 days you can list where time goes, agree simple ground rules for data and review, run one small pilot with the tools you already pay for, and measure it against a baseline. Then decide to scale, change or stop it.

Most small and mid-sized businesses don't need an AI strategy document to get started. They need one useful result, achieved safely, that the team trusts. The plan below gets you there in about 30 days, using tools you probably already pay for.

Why do so many businesses stall when they start with AI?

Usually because they start with a tool instead of a problem. Someone buys licences, a few people experiment, and three months later nobody can say what changed.

The gap is real. An OECD survey of more than 5,000 SMEs in seven countries found generative AI in use in 31% of them, and the OECD's paper on SME AI adoption notes that smaller firms still lag larger ones. Skills come up again and again as a barrier. In our experience the fix isn't more tools; it's a narrower first step.

Week 1: Where does the time actually go?

Before choosing anything, spend a week finding the work worth changing.

  1. Ask each team lead to list the five tasks that take the most hours each week.
  2. Mark which ones are repetitive, text-heavy or based on rules (answering the same questions, writing first drafts, copying data between systems, summarising documents).
  3. Estimate the hours per week for each, roughly. A rough number is enough to compare.
  4. Note any task that touches customer data, money or legal commitments. Those need extra care later.

By Friday you should have a short list of three to five candidates.

Week 2: Which problem should you pick first?

Choose one. A good first project is:

Test Good first project Poor first project
Frequency Happens daily or weekly Happens a few times a year
Effort Takes real hours today Takes minutes
Risk if AI gets it wrong A person reviews before anything goes out Errors reach customers or money directly
Data Uses information you already hold Needs data you don't have or can't share
Owner One person who wants it fixed Nobody in particular

Typical winners are drafting replies to common enquiries, summarising meetings or long documents, first drafts of proposals or reports, and pulling data from invoices or forms. Our AI Readiness Score takes about three minutes and shows which areas are ready to start.

Week 2 (continued): What ground rules do you need?

Agree a one-page set of rules before the pilot starts, not after. Keep it short:

  • Approved tools. Which AI tools staff may use for work, and which accounts (business accounts, not personal ones).
  • Data. What must never be pasted into an AI tool: customer personal data, passwords, confidential contracts, unless the tool is approved for it.
  • Review. Who checks AI output before it reaches a customer, and what they check.
  • Disclosure. When customers should be told AI was involved.
  • Owner. Who answers questions and updates the rules.

If you operate under data protection laws such as the GDPR or India's DPDP Act, check how your chosen tool stores and processes data. Our cybersecurity and data privacy practice covers this in more depth.

Week 3: How do you run a small pilot?

Run the pilot with two to five people for two weeks.

  1. Write down the baseline. How long the task takes now, and how often it needs rework.
  2. Use what you already have first. Microsoft 365 Copilot, Google Gemini or a business ChatGPT or Claude account often cover a first pilot without new systems.
  3. Create a shared prompt or template so everyone runs the task the same way, and improve it as you learn.
  4. Keep a simple log: time taken, what needed fixing, anything that went wrong.
  5. Check in twice a week for 15 minutes. Most improvements come from these conversations, not from the tool.

Week 4: How do you know if it worked?

Compare the pilot against the baseline, honestly.

Measure Question to answer
Time Did the task take less time, including review?
Quality Was the output as good, or better? How much rework was needed?
Risk Did anything go wrong, or nearly go wrong?
Adoption Do the people doing the work want to keep using it?

Then make one of three decisions: scale it to more people, change it and run another two weeks, or stop and pick the next problem from your list. Stopping is a good outcome too: you learned cheaply.

What comes after the first 30 days?

Once one pilot works, repeat the cycle with the next problem on your list. After two or three cycles you'll have real evidence about where AI helps your business, which makes a longer roadmap far easier to write and to trust.

If you'd like help with any step, our AI Strategy & Readiness practice runs short readiness scans and opportunity workshops, and our people and change practice trains teams to use AI well. Every engagement is scoped and quoted after a short call.

FAQ

Do we need an AI strategy before we start?

No. A short list of problems, a page of ground rules and one measured pilot will teach you more than a long strategy document. Write the strategy once you have evidence from two or three pilots.

Which AI tool should a small business start with?

Start with what you already pay for, such as Microsoft 365 Copilot, Google Gemini or a business account for ChatGPT or Claude. Choose a new tool only when a pilot shows a clear need the existing ones can't meet.

How much should we spend on a first AI pilot?

Keep it small: a few licences you may already have and a few hours a week of staff time for two weeks. The aim of a first pilot is learning, not a large investment.

What is the biggest risk when starting with AI?

Staff pasting sensitive data into unapproved tools, and AI output reaching customers without review. A one-page set of ground rules, agreed before the pilot, covers both.
